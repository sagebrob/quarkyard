2. Design Your Bottles

You're in charge of the setup. For each bottle, choose the number of fizzing tablets (0–5), the amount of water, and how far the bottle sits from the heat lamp (or a sunny window). The temperature results you get will depend entirely on what you choose here.

Real scientists usually change only one variable at a time between setups and keep everything else the same — that's what makes it possible to tell what actually caused a difference. You don't have to do that here. Try it however you want, and you'll be asked to think about the consequences of your choices later.

3. Run the Investigation

Press Start. The simulator will turn on all three heat sources and record the air temperature inside each bottle every 2 minutes for 20 minutes, using the setup you designed above.

Glossary

Concentration
How much of something (like CO2) is packed into a given amount of space. More CO2 molecules in the same size bottle = higher concentration.
Mixture
Two or more substances combined together, where each substance keeps its own properties (like air, which is a mixture of gases).
Solution
A mixture where one substance is fully dissolved evenly into another.
Greenhouse effect
The way certain gases in the atmosphere trap heat energy that would otherwise escape back into space, warming the air near Earth's surface.
Greenhouse gases
Gases (like CO2) that are especially good at trapping heat energy, causing the greenhouse effect.
System
The part of the world you're focused on studying — here, the water, air, and CO2 inside one sealed bottle.
Controlled variable
Something you deliberately keep the same across every trial, so it can't explain any differences in your results.
Independent variable
The one thing you deliberately change on purpose, to see what effect it has.
Dependent variable
What you measure, because you expect it to change depending on the independent variable.
