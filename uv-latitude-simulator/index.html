CASE FILE NO. UV-01 — SKIN CANCER INVESTIGATION

Who gets skin cancer, and why? A latitude & UV investigation

Skin cells mutate more when they absorb more UV radiation. If that's true, cities closer to the equator — where the sun is more direct — should have more melanoma (skin cancer). Real data from the CDC and NOAA can tell us whether that prediction actually holds up.

Driving question this connects to Who gets cancer, and why? Today you're testing one piece of that: does environment (UV exposure) predict who gets skin cancer, or is something more complicated going on?
Background — latitude and UV

Where on Earth is UV strongest?

Latitude is how far a place is from the equator. Near the equator, sunlight hits the ground more directly, so more UV reaches the ground. Toward the poles, sunlight arrives at a slant and passes through more air, so less UV gets through.

The shading is a simplified picture of average UV by latitude. Real UV also changes with elevation, season, clouds, and the ozone layer.

What "melanoma rate" means The melanoma rate (also called incidence) is the number of new melanoma cases in one year for every 100,000 people who live there. It is a rate, not a total count. That lets you fairly compare a place with many people to a place with few people.

Denver has been added to the chart and data table in Step 2 — scroll up to see exactly where it landed next to the other 11 locations. Notice that it's nowhere near Utah, despite almost identical elevation and UV Index.

Why isn't Denver's rate closer to Utah's? Elevation and UV Index at the city level don't capture two things that matter more:
  • Where people actually spend time outdoors. Denver's 5,280 ft is where most Coloradans live day to day. Utah's ski towns — Park City, Alta, Snowbird — sit 7,000–9,000+ ft, well above Denver, and a large share of Utahns recreate there regularly. Snow also reflects up to 80% of UV back upward, so a day skiing in Utah's mountains can deliver far more total UV dose than a day at Denver's elevation, even though both cities report similar numbers.
  • Who lives there. Utah's population is about 74.4% non-Hispanic White (U.S. Census), the highest share of any location in this dataset besides Vermont — a demographic pattern tied to the state's settlement history. Colorado's is 63.6% — still a majority, but a meaningfully more ethnically mixed population, including a larger Hispanic/Latino share than Utah's. Melanin (the pigment that helps protect skin from UV damage) varies within every population, but averaged across a whole state, that difference in composition pulls Colorado's statewide rate down even under nearly identical sunlight. You can check this yourself: switch the chart's X axis to "Population: Non-Hispanic White (%)" and see how closely it tracks the melanoma rate axis compared to elevation or UV Index.

A caution about this variable: "Non-Hispanic White" is a Census ethnicity category, not a direct skin-tone measurement — it lumps together people with a wide range of actual melanin levels, and it can make some places look misleading. Puerto Rico, for example, shows just 0.8% non-Hispanic White, but that's because the Census classifies most Puerto Ricans as Hispanic regardless of skin tone — not because the island's population is uniformly dark-skinned. Use this variable to spot a real pattern in the data, not to assume anything about individual people.

In other words: Denver's UV Index of 11 wasn't wrong. It just can't tell you where people actually stand under the sun, or whose skin is standing there. That's the same limitation you found with Utah, seen from the other direction.

Step 4 — Open the case file
Reveal: what else is going on here?

Latitude and UV Index affect how much UV radiation reaches the ground — but they don't fully explain who actually gets diagnosed with melanoma. A few other real factors are mixed in:

Elevation

UV radiation gets roughly 10–12% stronger for every 1,000 meters (about 3,280 feet) of elevation gain, because there's less atmosphere to filter it. That's part of why Utah, at an average elevation over a mile high, has a higher measured UV Index than its latitude alone would predict.

Skin tone and detection

Melanin (the pigment in skin) absorbs and scatters UV radiation, which is protective — people with more melanin in their skin have a lower baseline melanoma risk. Puerto Rico and Miami have larger Hispanic and Black populations than Utah or Vermont, which is a major reason their reported melanoma rates are lower despite far more direct sun. Melanoma is also sometimes caught later or under-diagnosed in patients with darker skin, since it's less commonly looked for there — so some of the reported difference reflects detection, not just biology.

Behavior

States like Utah and Vermont have high rates of outdoor recreation (skiing, hiking) at elevation, often with less consistent sun protection during colder-weather activities than people use at the beach. Coastal, hot-climate states often have stronger sun-safety norms — sunscreen, shade, covering up — precisely because the heat makes sun exposure more obviously uncomfortable.

What this means for "cause and effect"

UV radiation is still a real, well-established cause of the DNA mutations that lead to melanoma — that part of the science is solid. But "more UV in the environment" doesn't translate directly into "more diagnosed cases" in a simple straight line, because population (who lives there), behavior, and detection all interact with the biology. This is a common pattern in biology: a real cause can still produce a messy, non-obvious pattern in real-world data once other variables are mixed in.
